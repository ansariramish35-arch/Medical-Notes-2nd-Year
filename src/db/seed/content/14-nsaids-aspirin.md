## 1. Classification of NSAIDs (Complete)

| Sub-group | Members | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salicylates | **Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid)**, diflunisal | Aspirin irreversibly inhibits COX |
| Propionic acid derivatives | Ibuprofen, naproxen, ketoprofen | Most-used reversible NSAIDs |
| Acetic acid derivatives | Diclofenac, indomethacin, ketorolac | Indomethacin for gout/patent ductus; ketorolac for acute pain |
| Enolic acid (oxicams) | Piroxicam, meloxicam | Long half-life; meloxicam relatively COX-2 preferring |
| Fenamates | Mefenamic acid | Dysmenorrhoea |
| **Selective COX-2 inhibitors** | Celecoxib, etoricoxib | Less GI damage; cardiovascular caution |
| Para-aminophenol (analgesic-antipyretic, NOT anti-inflammatory) | **Paracetamol (acetaminophen)** | Weak peripheral COX inhibition; included for comparison |

**Prototype:** aspirin — the yardstick against which all NSAIDs are compared.

## 2. Mechanism of Action (Three Levels)

### Level 1 — One-line mechanism
NSAIDs inhibit **cyclo-oxygenase (COX)**, blocking conversion of arachidonic acid to **prostaglandins, prostacyclin and thromboxane** — thereby producing **analgesic, antipyretic and anti-inflammatory** effects.

### Level 2 — Step-by-step
1. Cell-membrane phospholipids → **arachidonic acid** (by phospholipase A2 — the step blocked by corticosteroids).
2. Arachidonic acid → two paths: **COX path** (prostaglandins, thromboxane) and **LOX path** (leukotrienes).
3. NSAIDs block COX: **aspirin irreversibly** (acetylates Ser530 of COX); other NSAIDs **reversibly** (competitive).
4. Consequences: ↓ PGE2 (pain, fever, inflammation), ↓ PGI2 (cytoprotection, renal flow), ↓ TXA2 (platelet aggregation).

### Level 3 — COX-1 vs COX-2
- **COX-1 (constitutive):** gastric cytoprotection, platelet TXA2, renal perfusion — the "housekeeping" isoform.
- **COX-2 (inducible):** expressed at sites of inflammation by cytokines — the "inflammatory" isoform.
- Classic NSAIDs block both → efficacy + toxicity; COX-2 selective drugs reduce GI toxicity but remove PGI2-mediated vascular protection.

:::why WHY? — Why does low-dose aspirin prevent MI, but ordinary NSAIDs do not?
Platelets are anucleate: they **cannot synthesize new COX**. Aspirin's **irreversible** acetylation disables platelet COX-1 for the platelet's entire 8–10 day life → TXA2 (a pro-aggregant) stays suppressed. Reversible NSAIDs block COX only transiently and may even compete with aspirin's binding — so the antiplatelet effect is aspirin's alone.
:::

:::why WHY? — Why does aspirin cause gastric bleeding?
PGE2/PGI2 normally (i) inhibit acid secretion, (ii) stimulate mucus–bicarbonate secretion and (iii) maintain mucosal blood flow. Blocking COX-1 removes all three protections → acid-peptic injury; aspirin additionally inhibits platelet TXA2, so any mucosal erosion bleeds longer. Two hits, one drug.
:::

## 3. Pharmacological Actions and Uses

| Action | Basis | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Analgesic | ↓ PGE2 sensitization of nociceptors | Headache, myalgia, dental pain |
| Antipyretic | ↓ PGE2 resetting of hypothalamic set-point raised by IL-1/TNF | Fever |
| Anti-inflammatory | ↓ vasodilator PGs at higher doses | Rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis |
| Antiplatelet | Irreversible TXA2 block | **Low-dose aspirin: secondary prevention of MI/stroke** |
| Uricosuric (high-dose only) | Blocks urate reabsorption | Historical gout use (rare now) |
| Closure of ductus arteriosus | PGs keep ductus open | Indomethacin in PDA |

## 4. Adverse Effects (with mechanisms)

| Adverse effect | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **GI: dyspepsia → ulcer → GI bleed** | COX-1 block → loss of PG cytoprotection + direct topical injury |
| Bleeding tendency | Platelet TXA2 block (aspirin irreversible) |
| Renal impairment, Na/water retention | Loss of renal PGI2/PGE2 vasodilation |
| Bronchospasm / aspirin asthma | Arachidonate shunted to LOX → leukotrienes |
| Reye syndrome | Aspirin in children with viral illness → hepatic encephalopathy — **avoid aspirin < 12–16 years** |
| Salicylism (overdose) | Tinnitus, hyperventilation (resp. alkalosis → metabolic acidosis) |
| Hypersensitivity | Urticaria, angioedema, anaphylaxis |

## 5. Aspirin vs Paracetamol (Difference-question ready)

| Feature | Aspirin | Paracetamol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anti-inflammatory | Yes | **Negligible** |
| Antiplatelet | Yes (irreversible) | No |
| GI toxicity | Significant | Minimal |
| Children | Avoid (Reye syndrome) | Safe |
| Overdose danger | Salicylism (acid-base disturbance) | **Hepatic necrosis** (NAPQI; antidote **N-acetylcysteine**) |
| Mechanism | Peripheral COX inhibition | Mainly central COX inhibition |

## 6. High-Yield and Exam Framework

:::highyield High-yield one-liners
- Aspirin = **irreversible** COX inhibitor; all other NSAIDs = reversible.
- COX-1 = housekeeping (stomach, platelets, kidney); COX-2 = inflammation.
- Low-dose aspirin → **antiplatelet** (TXA2); high dose → anti-inflammatory (PGE2).
- Steroids act higher up (phospholipase A2) — that is why they are more broadly immunosuppressive.
- Paracetamol overdose → **NAPQI** → treat with **N-acetylcysteine**.
- Do not give aspirin to children with viral fever (**Reye syndrome**).
:::
