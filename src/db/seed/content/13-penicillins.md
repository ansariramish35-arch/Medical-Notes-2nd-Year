## 1. Classification of Penicillins (Complete)

| Sub-group | Members | Key property |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Natural** | Benzylpenicillin (penicillin G), Phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) | Narrow spectrum; penicillinase-sensitive; G = parenteral, V = oral |
| **Penicillinase-resistant** | Cloxacillin, Dicloxacillin, Flucloxacillin, Methicillin | Bulky side chain blocks β-lactamase; anti-staphylococcal |
| **Aminopenicillins (broad spectrum)** | **Amoxicillin**, Ampicillin | Active against some Gram-negatives; penicillinase-sensitive |
| **Antipseudomonal** | Piperacillin (+ tazobactam), Ticarcillin, Carbenicillin | Extended Gram-negative cover incl. Pseudomonas |

- **Prototype:** benzylpenicillin (penicillin G).
- First β-lactam discovered by Alexander Fleming (1928, *Penicillium notatum*); chain production in the 1940s — mention in an introduction if the LAQ allows.

## 2. Mechanism of Action (Three Levels)

### Level 1 — One-line mechanism
Penicillins inhibit the **transpeptidation (cross-linking) step of peptidoglycan synthesis** by binding **penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)**, causing autolysin-mediated **osmotic lysis** — they are **bactericidal**.

### Level 2 — Step-by-step
1. The β-lactam ring structurally mimics the **D-Ala–D-Ala** terminus of the peptidoglycan precursor.
2. It binds and **acylates the active-site serine of PBPs** (transpeptidases, carboxypeptidases).
3. New cross-links cannot form → the growing wall is weak.
4. Simultaneously, autolytic enzymes (autolysins) keep cutting existing wall.
5. Wall synthesis < wall degradation → **osmotic lysis** of the bacterium.

### Level 3 — Why killing is selective and phase-dependent
- Mammalian cells have **no peptidoglycan** → exquisite selectivity (target absent in host).
- Only **actively dividing cells** build wall → penicillins are most effective in the **log phase** and ineffective against dormant organisms or those lacking a wall (Mycoplasma).

{{figure:penicillin-moa}}

```flow
β-lactam mimics D-Ala–D-Ala
↓
Binds PBPs (transpeptidases) → acyl-enzyme, irreversible
↓
Transpeptidation blocked → no new cross-links
↓
Autolysins continue degrading existing wall
↓
Weak wall + internal osmotic pressure → LYSIS (bactericidal)
```

## 3. Antibacterial Spectrum and Uses (with "WHY")

| Drug | Spectrum/use highlights | WHY it is chosen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Benzylpenicillin G | Streptococci, meningococci, syphilis, clostridia, leptospira | Drug of choice where sensitive; IV/IM only (acid-labile) |
| Penicillin V | Streptococcal pharyngitis (oral) | Acid-stable → oral |
| Procaine/benzathine penicillin | Rheumatic-fever prophylaxis; syphilis (benzathine) | Depot forms → prolonged low levels |
| Cloxacillin | Staphylococcal skin/soft-tissue infection | Resists staphylococcal β-lactamase |
| **Amoxicillin** | Community pneumonia, otitis media, UTI, *H. pylori* regimens | Oral bioavailability better than ampicillin |
| Piperacillin–tazobactam | Hospital-acquired / pseudomonal sepsis | Antipseudomonal + inhibitor protects the ring |

:::why WHY? — Why combine amoxicillin with clavulanic acid?
Clavulanic acid is a **β-lactamase inhibitor** (weak antibiotic itself): it binds and irreversibly inactivates β-lactamases, "sacrificing" itself so the penicillin survives to reach PBPs. The combination restores amoxicillin activity against β-lactamase-producing H. influenzae, Moraxella and staphylococci. Same logic: piperacillin + tazobactam; ampicillin + sulbactam.
:::

## 4. Adverse Effects (with mechanisms)

| Adverse effect | Mechanism | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hypersensitivity** — rash, urticaria, serum sickness, **anaphylaxis** (Type I) | IgE-mediated; cross-reactivity among penicillins | **Commonest cause of drug anaphylaxis**; always ask about allergy |
| Diarrhoea | Flora disturbance (esp. oral ampicillin) | Amoxicillin better tolerated than ampicillin |
| Interstitial nephritis | Hypersensitivity (esp. methicillin — historical) | Fever, eosinophilia, renal failure |
| Seizures at very high doses | GABA-A antagonism at high CSF levels | Renal failure risk factor |
| Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction (syphilis therapy) | Cytokine release from killed spirochaetes | Fever, rigors within hours of first dose |

## 5. Resistance (Enumeration — SAQ favourite)

1. **β-lactamase production** — hydrolysis of the β-lactam ring (commonest; staphylococci, H. influenzae, many Gram-negatives).
2. **Altered PBPs** — low drug affinity: **MRSA** (mecA gene → **PBP2a**); resistant pneumococci.
3. **Decreased permeability** — porin loss (Gram-negatives).
4. **Efflux pumps** — active removal of drug.

## 6. Comparison — Benzylpenicillin vs Amoxicillin (Difference-question ready)

| Feature | Benzylpenicillin (G) | Amoxicillin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Route | IV/IM only (acid-labile) | **Oral** (acid-stable, well absorbed) |
| Spectrum | Narrow (Gram-positive cocci, spirochaetes) | Broadened (adds some Gram-negatives) |
| β-lactamase stable | No | No (pair with clavulanate) |
| Classic uses | Meningococcal disease, syphilis, streptococcal sepsis | Otitis, sinusitis, CAP, UTI, H. pylori |
| Rash in EBV | Rare | **Classic maculopapular rash in infectious mononucleosis** |

## 7. High-Yield and Exam Framework

:::highyield High-yield one-liners
- MOA: **PBP binding → block transpeptidation → autolysin lysis**; bactericidal, log-phase dependent.
- **β-lactam ring** is the pharmacophore; β-lactamases destroy it; **clavulanate/sulbactam/tazobactam** protect it.
- MRSA resistance = **altered PBP (PBP2a, mecA)** — NOT β-lactamase.
- Commonest adverse effect = **hypersensitivity**; commonest serious = **anaphylaxis**.
- Methicillin is obsolete but lives on in the term **MRSA**.
:::
